For decades the message was simple: if you eat dairy, pick the low-fat version. Skimmed milk, fat-free yogurt, reduced-fat cheese. But a new study from the University of Toronto has people asking whether we got it wrong. Its headline finding – that three daily servings of full-fat dairy didn't cause weight gain or worse cholesterol – spread fast this month.

So can you go back to whole milk and proper cheese without guilt? The honest answer is "maybe, in sensible amounts". Here's what the study actually did, what it can and can't tell us, and how to make dairy work for you.

## What the new study did

The trial, published in *The Journal of Nutrition* and led by nutrition professor Harvey Anderson, followed **74 adults with overweight or obesity for 12 weeks**. Participants were split into three groups:

- A calorie-restricted diet with very little dairy (the comparison group)
- Three servings a day of full-fat dairy, with calories set to match what their bodies burned
- Three servings a day of full-fat dairy, with no specific calorie limit

Everyone got healthy-eating guidance based on Canada's food guide, and the researchers supplied the dairy foods.

## What it found

According to the university's summary, the full-fat dairy groups showed **no meaningful differences** from the low-dairy group in weight, body composition or blood cholesterol. Anderson said people eating three servings didn't show harmful cholesterol or blood fat levels, or signs of insulin resistance.

There were a few positives, too. The dairy groups had **better blood pressure** and took in more **calcium, protein and vitamin D** – nutrients many adults fall short on.

One possible explanation is something scientists call the **"dairy matrix"**. The idea is that the fat in cheese or yogurt doesn't act in the body exactly like the same amount of fat from, say, butter or processed meat, because it comes packaged with proteins (casein and whey), calcium and other nutrients that may change how it's digested. It's an interesting theory, but still being studied.

## Why you shouldn't rush to the cheese counter just yet

This is a useful study, but it's one small piece of a large puzzle. A few things to keep in mind:

**It was small and short.** 74 people for three months is a modest trial. The researchers themselves say it doesn't show whether eating full-fat dairy for years prevents or raises the risk of diabetes, heart disease or other long-term conditions.

**It was partly industry-funded.** The work was supported by a Canadian dairy research programme (along with a government-backed research fund). That doesn't make the results wrong, but it's worth knowing, and it's one more reason to wait for independent studies that confirm it.

**Saturated fat still matters.** The American Heart Association says eating too much saturated fat can raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol, and recommends keeping it under about 6% of daily calories – roughly 13 grams a day on a 2,000-calorie diet. Full-fat cheese, butter, cream and ice cream are among the main sources. The NHS likewise points out that much of the fat in dairy is saturated, and still advises most adults to choose lower-fat options.

**What you swap matters.** Harvard's Nutrition Source summarises long-term research showing that dairy fat itself wasn't clearly linked to heart disease in some big studies – but replacing dairy fat with **vegetable oils or other unsaturated fats** was linked to lower heart risk. In other words, full-fat dairy may be fine compared with refined carbs or processed meat, but olive oil, nuts and seeds still look better for your heart.

## So, full-fat or low-fat?

Here's a balanced way to think about it. The latest evidence suggests full-fat dairy probably isn't the villain it was made out to be – especially fermented foods like plain yogurt and cheese. But it also isn't a health food you should eat without limits. Your overall diet matters far more than whether your milk is 1% or whole.

Some practical guidelines:

- **Enjoy dairy in moderation.** Guidelines differ: the US Dietary Guidelines suggest about three servings a day, while Harvard's Healthy Eating Plate suggests limiting dairy to one or two. A sensible middle ground for most people is one to three servings.
- **Favour plain, fermented options.** Natural yogurt, kefir and good-quality cheese bring protein, calcium and (in yogurt and kefir) live cultures. Flavoured yogurts and dairy desserts often hide a lot of sugar.
- **Choose full-fat if it keeps you satisfied.** If a smaller portion of creamy Greek yogurt stops you snacking an hour later, that may be a better choice than a bigger tub of sugary fat-free yogurt.
- **Watch the extras.** Butter, cream and ice cream are much higher in saturated fat and offer fewer nutrients than milk or yogurt. Keep them as occasional treats.
- **Balance the rest of your plate.** If you enjoy full-fat dairy, get most of your other fats from olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado and oily fish.
- **Mind the cheese portions.** A serving of hard cheese is about the size of two dice (around 30 g). It's easy to eat three times that on a cheese board.

> **Tip:** Want the creaminess without going overboard? Stir a spoonful of full-fat Greek yogurt into soups, porridge or curries instead of cream – you'll get more protein and less saturated fat.

## Who should be more careful

If you have **high cholesterol, heart disease or a strong family history** of either, or you've been told to limit saturated fat, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before switching to full-fat dairy. Some people's cholesterol responds strongly to saturated fat, and one short study shouldn't override personal medical advice. The same goes for anyone managing their weight closely – full-fat dairy is more calorie-dense, so portions count.

## The bottom line

The new Toronto study adds to growing evidence that full-fat dairy, eaten in moderate amounts, isn't as harmful as we once believed – and may even bring benefits like better blood pressure and more calcium and protein. But it was small, short and partly industry-funded, and major health bodies still advise keeping saturated fat in check. Enjoy plain yogurt, milk and cheese in sensible portions, pick the version you enjoy and that keeps you full, and let the rest of your plate be rich in plants and unsaturated fats.
