If you've scrolled through TikTok lately, you've probably met "fibermaxxing": people piling chia seeds onto yogurt, tipping beans into everything and proudly logging their daily fibre grams. Retail reports this year even name fibre as one of the fastest-growing wellness categories. For once, a viral food trend is pointing in a genuinely useful direction. Most of us really don't eat enough fibre.

But "maxxing" anything can backfire, and fibre is no exception. Below, we look at what the evidence actually says, how much you need, and how to get more without a bloated, uncomfortable week. Then we'll make a big pot of lentil and bean soup — one of the easiest, cosiest ways to close the fibre gap as the evenings get colder.

## What the fibermaxxing trend gets right

The core message is solid. Health bodies on both sides of the Atlantic agree that fibre matters and that most people fall short.

- **The gap is real.** UCLA Health notes that more than 90% of women and 97% of men in the US don't hit the recommended intake. Harvard's Nutrition Source puts the average American intake at about 15 grams a day, and the NHS says most UK adults manage about 20 grams.
- **The benefits are well documented.** The NHS links a fibre-rich diet to a lower risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer, as well as easier digestion. UCLA Health adds lower cholesterol, steadier blood sugar and feeling fuller after meals.
- **Different fibres do different jobs.** *Soluble* fibre (in oats, beans, apples and carrots) forms a gel that can help lower LDL cholesterol. *Insoluble* fibre (in wholegrains, nuts and many vegetables) keeps things moving through your gut. Eating a variety of plants gets you both.

## Where "maxxing" goes too far

The trouble starts when more becomes the goal in itself.

**Going from zero to hero overnight.** UCLA Health warns that jumping from a low-fibre diet to a very high one can cause gas, bloating and even constipation, especially if you don't drink enough water. A gradual increase over a couple of weeks is kinder to your gut.

**Leaning on powders and supplements.** Harvard's Nutrition Source suggests using supplements like psyllium only if food alone isn't enough. Whole foods give you fibre plus vitamins, minerals and plant compounds that a scoop of powder can't match.

**Forgetting that everyone is different.** If you live with IBS, Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, UCLA Health advises taking extra care with fibre and getting personal advice. If you have any digestive condition, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before making big changes.

> **The friendlier goal:** Think "fibre-meeting", not fibre-maxxing. Aim to reach the recommended amount steadily, from a mix of foods, rather than chasing the highest number possible.

## How much fibre do you actually need?

Advice varies a little by country:

- **UK (NHS):** 30 grams a day for adults.
- **US (HHS, via UCLA Health):** about 25 grams for women and 38 grams for men.
- **Harvard Nutrition Source:** at least 25–35 grams a day.

To picture it, UCLA Health lists one cup of lentil soup at around 8 grams, a cup of raspberries at 8 grams and half an avocado at 5 grams. A bowl of the soup below, packed with lentils, beans and vegetables, gets you a big step closer.

## Hearty lentil, white bean and vegetable soup

**Serves:** 4 generous bowls
**Prep time:** 15 minutes
**Cook time:** 35 minutes
**Fibre:** roughly 12 g per serving (our estimate, based on typical values for the ingredients)

### Ingredients

- 2 tablespoons olive oil
- 1 large onion, finely chopped
- 2 carrots, diced (no need to peel — the skin adds fibre)
- 2 celery sticks, diced
- 3 garlic cloves, crushed
- 1 teaspoon ground cumin
- 1 teaspoon smoked paprika
- ½ teaspoon ground turmeric
- 200 g red lentils, rinsed
- 1 tin (400 g) chopped tomatoes
- 1.2 litres low-salt vegetable stock
- 1 tin (400 g) cannellini or butter beans, drained and rinsed
- 2 big handfuls of spinach or chopped kale (about 100 g)
- Juice of ½ lemon
- Salt and black pepper, to taste

**To serve (optional):** a spoonful of plain yogurt, chopped parsley, a sprinkle of pumpkin seeds and a slice of wholemeal or seeded bread.

### Method

1. **Soften the vegetables.** Warm the olive oil in a large pot over a medium heat. Add the onion, carrots and celery with a pinch of salt and cook for 8–10 minutes, stirring now and then, until soft and lightly golden.
2. **Add the flavour.** Stir in the garlic, cumin, paprika and turmeric and cook for 1 minute, until fragrant.
3. **Simmer.** Add the lentils, chopped tomatoes and stock. Bring to the boil, then lower the heat and simmer, partly covered, for 20 minutes, until the lentils have broken down and gone soft.
4. **Add the beans and greens.** Stir in the drained beans and the spinach or kale. Simmer for another 5 minutes, until the beans are hot and the greens have wilted.
5. **Finish.** Squeeze in the lemon juice, then taste and season with salt and pepper. For a creamier texture, blend about a third of the soup and stir it back in. If it's too thick, add a splash of hot water.

### Variations and storage

- **Make it heartier:** stir in a handful of cooked pearl barley or wholewheat pasta.
- **Add a little heat:** a pinch of chilli flakes or a spoonful of harissa works beautifully.
- **Swap the beans:** chickpeas or borlotti beans are just as good.
- **Storage:** keeps for 4 days in the fridge and up to 3 months in the freezer. It thickens as it sits, so loosen it with water when reheating.

## Easy ways to add fibre to the rest of your day

You don't need to overhaul everything. A few swaps, mostly suggested by the NHS and Harvard, add up quickly:

- **Breakfast:** choose porridge or a wholegrain cereal and top it with berries or a spoonful of ground flaxseed.
- **Bread and grains:** swap to wholemeal bread, brown rice or wholewheat pasta.
- **Potatoes:** leave the skins on.
- **Snacks:** pick whole fruit over juice, or try veggie sticks, oatcakes or a small handful of unsalted nuts.
- **Meat-free nights:** Harvard suggests using beans or lentils instead of meat two or three times a week — this soup counts!
- **Read labels:** the NHS describes foods with 6 g or more of fibre per 100 g as high-fibre.

And whatever you add, add water too, and give your body a couple of weeks to adjust.

## The bottom line

Fibermaxxing is one of the rare viral trends with real science behind it: most people eat far less fibre than they need, and getting more is linked to a healthier heart, gut and blood sugar. The smart version isn't about extremes or powders, though. Build up gradually, drink plenty of water and get your fibre from a colourful mix of whole foods. A pot of lentil and bean soup on a chilly evening is a delicious place to start.
