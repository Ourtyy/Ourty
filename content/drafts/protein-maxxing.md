Protein pancakes. Protein coffee. Protein chips. Even protein water. Walk down any supermarket aisle in 2026 and it feels like everything has been "boosted". On social media, "protein-maxxing" — packing as many grams into your day as possible — has become a lifestyle.

Stanford researcher Marily Oppezzo has compared the hype to how low-fat foods were marketed in the 1990s. So is all this extra protein doing you any good? Let's look at what you actually need, who might benefit from a bit more, and where your protein should come from.

## Why protein matters

Protein isn't just for bodybuilders. Your body uses it to build and repair muscles, bones, skin and organs, and to make enzymes and hormones. Getting enough helps you stay strong as you age and can keep you feeling full after meals.

So protein really is important. The question is how much is enough — and whether more is always better.

## How much protein do you need?

This is where it gets a little confusing, because the numbers have shifted.

- **The classic minimum:** For years, the official recommendation (RDA) has been **0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day**. Harvard's Nutrition Source explains that's about 50 grams for a 140-lb (64 kg) person and 70 grams for a 200-lb (91 kg) person. It's a minimum to stay healthy, not an ideal target.
- **The newer US guidance:** As NPR reported, the updated US Dietary Guidelines now suggest **1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram per day**. For a 150-lb (68 kg) person, that works out to roughly **82 to 109 grams a day**.

### Work out your own range

1. Take your weight in kilograms (or divide your weight in pounds by 2.2).
2. Multiply by 1.2 for the low end and 1.6 for the high end.

So someone weighing 60 kg would aim for roughly 72–96 grams a day.

### Here's the twist: most people already get enough

According to Stanford Medicine, national survey data shows American men eat around 90–100 grams of protein a day and women about 65–75 grams. That already meets the newer recommendations for most people. Stanford nutrition scientist Christopher Gardner points out that the underlying science hasn't changed much, even though the guidelines have.

In other words, if you eat a varied diet with regular meals, you're probably doing fine without protein bars and shakes.

## Who might need a bit more?

Some people do benefit from aiming for the higher end of the range:

- **Adults over 40 or so.** Muscle loss often begins in your 30s and 40s, even if you don't notice it until later. McMaster University researcher Stuart Phillips told NPR that protein plus strength training is what slows it down.
- **People who exercise regularly**, especially with weights. Activity is "one of the key drivers" of how much protein you need, according to Phillips.
- **People losing weight**, including those on weight-loss medications. Extra protein may help limit the muscle you lose along with fat, Stanford's Oppezzo explains.
- **Women**, who on average tend to eat less protein and do less strength training than men.

> **The real secret:** Experts agree that protein alone won't build muscle. Oppezzo calls the effect of extra protein "pretty small potatoes" compared with strength training. If you want to stay strong, lifting, squatting and carrying matter far more than an extra shake.

## Where your protein comes from matters more than how much

This might be the most useful part of the whole debate. Harvard's Nutrition Source talks about the protein **"package"**: the fat, fibre, salt and other nutrients that come along with it. In a Harvard analysis of more than 130,000 people, the *amount* of protein people ate wasn't linked to their risk of dying early, but the *source* was.

**Great choices:**
- Beans, lentils, chickpeas and peas
- Tofu, tempeh and edamame
- Nuts and seeds
- Fish and seafood
- Poultry and eggs
- Plain yogurt

**Eat less often:**
- Red meat (beef, pork, lamb)

**Best avoided:**
- Processed meats like bacon, sausages, ham and cold cuts. The World Health Organization's cancer agency classifies processed meat as a cause of bowel cancer.

There's a bonus to choosing plants. Stanford's Gardner notes that relying on steak and shakes can crowd out fibre, and only about 5% of Americans get enough of it. Beans, lentils and whole grains give you protein *and* fibre at the same time. And no, you don't need to carefully combine plant foods at every meal: if you eat a variety through the day, you'll get all the amino acids you need.

## Easy, tasty ways to hit your target

You don't need special products. Using figures from NPR, here's what common foods provide:

- **1 cup of Greek yogurt:** about 17–20 g
- **1 cup of cooked lentils:** about 18 g
- **4 oz (113 g) chicken breast:** about 35 g
- **A small handful of nuts:** about 4–5 g

A simple day might look like Greek yogurt with berries and seeds for breakfast, a lentil soup or chickpea salad for lunch, a handful of almonds as a snack, and salmon or tofu with vegetables and brown rice for dinner. That easily lands you in the recommended range, with plenty of fibre on the side.

### What about protein powders and bars?

They can be handy if you're busy or have higher needs, but they're not essential. Harvard notes that protein powders aren't regulated by the FDA in the same way as medicines, and some contain added sugars or other additives, so read the label. Whole foods give you vitamins, minerals and fibre that a scoop of powder can't.

## A few cautions

- **Kidney or liver disease:** People with chronic kidney disease may need *less* protein, not more. Follow your doctor's advice.
- **Big changes:** If you're thinking of dramatically increasing your protein, check with your doctor or a registered dietitian first.
- **Saturated fat:** If most of your protein comes from meat and full-fat dairy, keep an eye on saturated fat.

## The bottom line

Protein matters, but the protein-maxxing trend oversells it. Most people already eat enough, and the extra grams in a protein cookie won't transform your health. Aim for roughly 1.2–1.6 g per kilogram if you're active or over 40, choose mostly beans, lentils, fish, poultry, eggs, yogurt and nuts, and pair it all with regular strength training. That combination, not a fancy protein water, is what keeps you strong for years to come.
