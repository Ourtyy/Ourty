Few things feel as good as a cosy afternoon nap. But a study that has been doing the rounds this month carried an alarming headline: napping linked to a higher risk of death.

Before you feel guilty about your Sunday snooze, it's worth looking closer. The study doesn't say that naps are bad for you. What it suggests is more interesting: *how* an older person naps may be a quiet signal about their health. Here's what the research found, and how to nap in a way that actually helps.

## What the study looked at

The research was published in *JAMA Network Open* by scientists at Mass General Brigham and Rush University Medical Center. It used data from the Rush Memory and Aging Project, a long-running study of older adults in Illinois, and it got fresh attention in November 2026 when it was featured by ScienceDaily.

Here's what makes it stand out:

- **1,338 older adults** took part, aged 56 and over, with an average age of about 81.
- Instead of asking people how much they napped, the researchers measured it with a **wrist-worn activity monitor** for up to two weeks. Any sleep between 9 a.m. and 7 p.m. counted as a nap.
- People were followed for **up to 19 years**, and the researchers looked at who died during that time.

Naps were common: almost everyone in the study napped at least a little. The typical person slept just under an hour during the day, spread over a couple of naps.

## The main findings

After accounting for things like age, health conditions and night-time sleep, the researchers found:

- **Longer naps:** each extra hour of daytime sleep was linked to about a **13% higher risk** of dying during the study.
- **More frequent naps:** each extra nap per day was linked to about a **7% higher risk**.
- **Morning naps:** people who usually napped in the morning had about a **30% higher risk** than those who napped in the early afternoon.
- **Irregular naps:** napping at different times or for different lengths from day to day was *not* linked to higher risk.

## Correlation, not causation

This is the most important part. The lead author, Dr Chenlu Gao, stressed that the results show a correlation, not cause and effect. In other words, the naps probably aren't harming people. Instead, needing a lot of daytime sleep may be an **early sign of something else**, such as an underlying illness, a chronic condition, disturbed night-time sleep or a body clock that's out of sync.

The study's own details support this. When the researchers removed people who already had memory or thinking problems at the start, the link with nap length became weaker and was no longer statistically clear. That suggests some of the risk is explained by health problems that were already there.

The authors describe long, frequent and morning naps as a possible **"behavioral marker"** of risk in later life. They hope wearable devices could one day help spot these patterns early, so doctors can look for the cause sooner.

There are other limits too. Most participants were older, white women in one part of the US, and naps were measured only once at the start. The findings may not apply to younger adults, shift workers or new parents catching up on sleep.

> **In short:** a short afternoon nap is not a health risk. But if you or an older relative suddenly need much more daytime sleep than usual, it's worth paying attention.

## What a healthy nap looks like

For most adults, a well-timed nap can boost alertness and mood. The Sleep Foundation offers some simple guidelines:

- **Keep it short.** About 20 minutes, and no longer than 30, is ideal for most adults. Longer naps can leave you groggy, because waking from deep sleep can make you feel worse, not better.
- **Time it well.** The best time is just before or during the natural post-lunch dip. Experts suggest napping at least eight hours before bedtime, which for most people means **before 3 p.m.**
- **Protect your night.** In older adults, daytime napping is linked with more night-time waking. If you struggle to sleep at night, try cutting back on naps first.

A few practical extras help too: set an alarm, nap somewhere comfortable but not *too* cosy (a sofa often works better than getting into bed), and get some daylight and movement afterwards to shake off any sleepiness.

## When daytime sleepiness needs a doctor

Sometimes the need to nap is your body telling you something. According to the NHS, common causes of ongoing tiredness include poor sleep or insomnia, stress, low mood, hormonal changes and illness. It also lists medical conditions such as **sleep apnoea, iron deficiency anaemia, diabetes and thyroid problems**.

The NHS suggests seeing a GP if:

- you've felt tired for a few weeks and don't know why
- tiredness is affecting your daily life
- you have other symptoms, like weight loss or mood changes
- someone has told you that you gasp, snort or choke in your sleep

The Sleep Foundation adds that falling asleep unexpectedly or at times you don't want to is different from choosing to nap, and may point to a condition worth checking.

For older relatives, a noticeable change, such as starting to nap every morning or sleeping much more during the day than they used to, is a good reason to mention it at their next check-up.

## The bottom line

A large 19-year study found that older adults who took longer, more frequent or morning naps had a higher risk of dying during the study period. But the naps themselves probably aren't the problem: they are more likely a sign of underlying health issues or poor night-time sleep.

For most people, a short nap of 20 to 30 minutes in the early afternoon is a perfectly healthy habit. What matters is noticing changes. If you, or someone you love, suddenly needs a lot more daytime sleep, don't just put it down to age: talk to a doctor.
