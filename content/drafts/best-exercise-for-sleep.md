If you lie awake at night, you've probably heard the advice to "get more exercise". But which kind actually helps most? A brisk walk, a spin class, lifting weights, a slow stretch before bed? A new review published this month tried to answer exactly that question – and the winner surprised a lot of people.

Researchers pooled 30 trials and found that **yoga** came out on top for people with poor sleep, ahead of walking and even regular aerobic exercise. Here's what the study found, how much weight to give it, and how to use movement to sleep better tonight.

## What the new review found

The analysis, published in the journal *Sleep and Biological Rhythms* by researchers at Harbin Sport University in China, combined **30 randomised controlled trials involving more than 2,500 adults** with sleep problems, from around a dozen countries. It was a "network meta-analysis", a method that lets scientists compare several types of exercise against each other even when they weren't all tested in the same trial.

The exercise types compared included yoga, walking, aerobic exercise, resistance training, combined programmes and traditional Chinese practices such as tai chi and qigong. According to ScienceAlert's coverage:

- **Yoga ranked first** for improving sleep quality
- **Walking came second**
- **Resistance training** (including Pilates and weights) came third
- Yoga was **significantly better than aerobic exercise** in the comparisons

The most effective "prescription" the researchers identified was yoga done **twice a week for 8–10 weeks, in sessions of 30 minutes or less, at a high intensity**. That's a surprise for anyone who pictures yoga as slow stretching. "High intensity" here means a more active, flowing style that gets your heart rate up – not a gentle wind-down routine.

## Why might yoga help?

The review didn't test *why* yoga worked best, but it's easy to see why it's a good candidate. Yoga combines physical effort with controlled breathing and focused attention, which may help calm a racing mind – one of the most common reasons people can't drop off. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) notes that yoga has been shown to help sleep in several groups, including older adults, women with sleep problems and people living with cancer.

## How much should you trust it?

This is encouraging, but it's not the final word. The authors themselves urged caution because the number of studies was limited and people with sleep problems are a varied group. A few more things to bear in mind:

- **Rankings can be fragile.** In a network meta-analysis, the gap between first and third place can be small and can shift as new trials are added.
- **Walking and strength training still worked.** The big message isn't "only yoga counts" – it's that several kinds of exercise helped. Choose the one you'll actually stick with.
- **It's about poor sleepers.** Results may not apply in the same way to people who already sleep well, or to medical conditions such as sleep apnoea.

Other recent research points the same way. A University of Texas study of students wearing Fitbits found that as little as **10 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity** was linked to better sleep, and that people who moved on more days had more deep, restorative sleep. It was observational, so it can't prove exercise caused the better sleep – but it fits the bigger picture that regular movement and good rest go together.

## When to exercise for better sleep

Timing matters a bit too. Advice from the NHS and the Sleep Foundation boils down to this:

- **There's no single best time of day.** Morning and afternoon exercise may help you settle into an earlier sleep schedule.
- **Evening exercise is fine for most people**, but vigorous workouts right before bed can make it harder for some to fall asleep, because your body needs time to cool down. The Sleep Foundation suggests finishing moderate exercise at least **90 minutes before bedtime**.
- **If you have insomnia, leave a bigger gap.** The NHS advises exercising regularly during the day but not in the **4 hours before bed**.

## A simple plan to try

You don't need a fancy studio membership. Here's an easy way to put the research into practice:

1. **Add two short, active yoga sessions a week.** Look for a 20–30 minute "vinyasa" or "power yoga" video – something that gets you a little warm and out of breath. Do it in the morning or late afternoon.
2. **Walk on most other days.** Even 10–15 brisk minutes counts, and daylight in the morning is a bonus for your body clock.
3. **Add some strength work.** Bodyweight squats, lunges or Pilates once or twice a week round things out and support long-term health.
4. **Keep the evening gentle.** If you want to move before bed, choose slow stretching or a few minutes of calm breathing instead of an intense session.
5. **Give it time.** The trials ran for 8–10 weeks. Don't give up after a few nights.

> **Tip:** Aim for the overall goal of about 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, the standard adult guideline the NHS recommends. Sleep benefits tend to come with consistency, not one heroic workout.

## Stay safe

Yoga is generally safe for healthy people when done properly, but the NCCIH notes that injuries – mostly sprains and strains – are more common in people over 65. If you're new, start with a qualified instructor or beginner class, skip advanced poses like headstands, and be careful with hot yoga, which carries risks of overheating and dehydration. If you're pregnant, older, or living with a health condition, check with your doctor first.

And if you've had trouble sleeping for months, or it's affecting your daily life, don't just push through. Exercise helps, but the NHS recommends seeing your GP – effective treatments such as cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) are available.

## The bottom line

A new review of 30 trials suggests that short, energetic yoga sessions twice a week may be the most effective exercise for poor sleep, with walking and strength training close behind. The evidence is still limited, so think of yoga as a great option rather than a magic cure. The real secret is regular movement you enjoy, done earlier in the day, alongside good sleep habits – and a chat with your doctor if sleepless nights don't improve.
