Every night, thousands of people stick a strip of tape across their lips before bed. Scroll through TikTok or Instagram and you'll find them promising deeper sleep, less snoring, fresher breath and even a sharper jawline. Mouth tape is now sold in pharmacies and pushed by celebrities and influencers.

It sounds simple, cheap and harmless. But is it? We looked at what the research actually says, and the answer is more cautious than your feed suggests.

## What is mouth taping?

Mouth taping means sealing your lips with a small piece of tape or a special strip at night, so you're encouraged to breathe through your nose while you sleep. The idea isn't completely out of nowhere. Your nose is built for breathing: it warms, moistens and filters the air before it reaches your lungs.

Breathing through your mouth all night, on the other hand, is linked to dry mouth, bad breath, gum problems and more snoring. So the logic goes: close the mouth, force the nose to do its job, and sleep better.

## What the trend promises

Fans of mouth taping say it can:

- Reduce snoring
- Improve sleep quality
- Help with dry mouth and morning breath
- Support dental health
- Even lower blood pressure or shape the jawline

That's a long list. Here's how it holds up.

## What the research actually shows

### The evidence is small and patchy

According to Cleveland Clinic, most of the research on mouth taping involves very small groups of people. One study of 30 people found less snoring after taping. Another study of 36 people with asthma found no change in their condition. In a 2022 study of just 10 people, some participants kept trying to breathe through their mouths anyway — puffing their cheeks against the tape.

### The bigger reviews aren't convinced

In 2025, two reviews pulled together the available studies, as reported by PolitiFact:

- A **systematic review in *PLOS One*** looked at 10 studies with 213 patients in total. It found no support for mouth taping as a treatment for people with sleep-disordered breathing in general. The quality of the evidence was mostly rated "uncertain" or "minimal". Some studies did find small improvements in snoring and in *mild* sleep apnea, but that's as far as it goes.
- A **review in the *American Journal of Otolaryngology*** examined nine studies and found that few of the claims made on TikTok had ever been tested.

A Cleveland Clinic expert summed it up simply: there isn't strong enough evidence to say mouth tape is beneficial. Most of the support is anecdotal — people saying it worked for them.

> **In short:** Mouth taping might quiet snoring a little for some people. There's no good evidence that it improves sleep for most of us, and the jawline and blood pressure claims are largely untested.

## The risks nobody mentions on TikTok

This is where it gets important. Doctors interviewed by PolitiFact pointed out that the people most drawn to mouth taping — chronic mouth breathers and heavy snorers — are often the ones most at risk from it.

**It can hide a real problem.** Mouth breathing and snoring are often signs of something else: a blocked nose, allergies, a deviated septum or sleep apnea. If you tape over the symptom, you may never get the cause checked. Untreated sleep apnea is linked to fatigue, headaches, trouble concentrating and strain on the heart.

**It can be dangerous if your nose is blocked.** If you can't breathe well through your nose, breathing through your mouth is your body's backup plan. The *PLOS One* review warned of "potential serious detrimental health outcomes" for people with nasal obstruction. There's also a risk if you were to vomit during the night with your mouth taped.

**It can simply make sleep worse.** Cleveland Clinic lists skin irritation or allergic reactions on the lips, anxiety, difficulty breathing and trouble falling or staying asleep as possible downsides.

### Who should not try mouth taping

Based on Cleveland Clinic's advice, skip it if you have:

- A blocked or stuffy nose, chronic allergies or a sinus infection
- A deviated septum or enlarged tonsils
- Known or suspected sleep apnea
- Heart problems

And if you've always been a mouth breather, that's a reason to see a doctor, not to reach for the tape.

## Safer ways to breathe (and sleep) easier

If snoring or a dry mouth is bothering you, there are gentler options with less risk. Cleveland Clinic suggests:

- **Sleep on your side.** Lying on your back makes snoring more likely.
- **Try nasal strips.** These open your nostrils slightly to help air flow.
- **Treat congestion.** If allergies or a cold are blocking your nose, ask a pharmacist about suitable remedies.
- **Get checked for sleep apnea.** If you have it, proven treatments such as a CPAP machine or an oral appliance can make a big difference.

A few everyday habits help, too: keep your bedroom cool and well ventilated, stay hydrated during the day, and stick to a regular sleep schedule.

> **Tip:** Ask your partner (or use a free snoring app) to notice whether you snore loudly, gasp or seem to stop breathing at night. These are classic signs of sleep apnea and worth mentioning to your doctor.

## When to see a doctor

Talk to a doctor before trying mouth taping, especially if you:

- Snore loudly most nights
- Wake up tired even after a full night's sleep
- Have morning headaches or feel very sleepy during the day
- Have been told you stop breathing or gasp in your sleep
- Can't breathe comfortably through your nose

A doctor can look for the reason behind your mouth breathing or snoring and treat it properly. As one sleep specialist told PolitiFact, fixing the cause is better than taping over it.

## The bottom line

Mouth taping is a classic viral trend: a simple idea, a grain of truth (nose breathing really is good for you) and a lot of hype. The research so far is small, mixed and mostly unconvincing, and for people with a blocked nose or undiagnosed sleep apnea, it can be risky. If you're curious and your nose is completely clear, talk to your doctor first. If you snore, feel exhausted or always breathe through your mouth, skip the tape and get checked out. Better sleep starts with finding out *why* you're not sleeping well.
