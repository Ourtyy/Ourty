For years, kale got all the attention. Now the humble cabbage is stealing the spotlight. Pinterest named a "Cabbage Crush" one of its big food trends for 2026, with searches for things like cabbage dumplings and cabbage soups climbing sharply. And honestly? It makes sense. Cabbage is cheap, it keeps for weeks in the fridge, it's at its best in the colder months, and when you roast it, something magical happens: the edges turn crisp and sweet, almost like a crunchy chip.

This recipe is the easiest way to join the trend. Thick wedges of cabbage are roasted until golden, then served with a quick lemon-garlic yogurt sauce. It works as a side for chicken or fish, or as a light vegetarian dinner with some beans or grains on the side.

## Why cabbage deserves a place on your plate

Cabbage belongs to the cruciferous family, alongside broccoli, kale, Brussels sprouts and bok choy. Here's what reliable sources say about it — and where the evidence stops.

- **It's light but filling.** According to Harvard Health, half a cup of cabbage has only about 14 calories, yet it's a good source of fibre.
- **It's rich in vitamin C.** That same half-cup gives you roughly 45% of the daily recommended amount, whether you choose green, red or Savoy cabbage.
- **Red cabbage has extra colour for a reason.** Its purple shade comes from anthocyanins, plant pigments that population studies have linked to a lower risk of heart disease. That's an association, though, not proof that cabbage itself protects your heart.
- **Cruciferous veggies are worth eating regularly.** Cleveland Clinic notes that diets rich in these vegetables have been associated with lower rates of several cancers in research. Again, these are promising findings rather than guarantees — think of cabbage as one helpful part of a varied, plant-rich diet, not a superfood cure.

> **Tip:** Most adults should aim for about 2.5 cups of vegetables a day, according to Cleveland Clinic. Two cabbage wedges go a long way towards that goal.

## Crispy roasted cabbage wedges with lemon-garlic yogurt

**Serves:** 4 as a side, 2 as a main
**Prep time:** 10 minutes
**Cook time:** 25–30 minutes

### Ingredients

**For the cabbage:**
- 1 medium green or Savoy cabbage (about 1 kg)
- 3 tablespoons olive oil
- 1 teaspoon smoked paprika
- ½ teaspoon garlic powder
- ½ teaspoon salt and a good pinch of black pepper
- Optional: 2 tablespoons grated Parmesan or a pinch of chilli flakes

**For the lemon-garlic yogurt:**
- 150 g plain Greek yogurt
- 1 small garlic clove, finely grated
- Zest of ½ lemon and 1 tablespoon of the juice
- 1 tablespoon chopped fresh dill or parsley
- A pinch of salt

**To finish:**
- A handful of chopped parsley
- 1 tablespoon toasted sunflower or pumpkin seeds

### Method

1. **Heat the oven** to 220°C (200°C fan / 425°F) and line a large baking tray with baking paper.
2. **Cut the cabbage.** Remove any tired outer leaves. Stand the cabbage on its base and cut it in half through the core, then cut each half into 3–4 wedges about 3 cm thick. Keep a bit of the core on each wedge — it holds the leaves together.
3. **Season.** Mix the olive oil, paprika, garlic powder, salt and pepper in a small bowl. Lay the wedges flat on the tray and brush the mixture over both sides, letting it drip between the leaves.
4. **Roast.** Roast for 15 minutes, then carefully flip each wedge with a spatula. If you're using Parmesan, sprinkle it on now. Roast for another 10–15 minutes, until the edges are deep golden and crispy and the centre is tender when pierced with a knife.
5. **Make the sauce.** While the cabbage roasts, stir together the yogurt, garlic, lemon zest and juice, herbs and salt. Taste and add a little more lemon if you like it tangy.
6. **Serve.** Spoon the yogurt onto a plate, arrange the hot wedges on top, and scatter over parsley and toasted seeds.

## Tips for the crispiest wedges

- **Don't crowd the tray.** Wedges that touch will steam instead of roast. Use two trays if needed.
- **Go hot.** A high oven temperature is what gives you those caramelised, chip-like edges.
- **Dry the cabbage.** If you rinse it, pat the wedges dry before oiling them — water is the enemy of crunch.
- **Charred bits are fine, burnt isn't.** Some dark edges add flavour, but keep an eye on the tray in the last five minutes.

## Easy variations

Once you've made the basic version, it's easy to change things up:

- **Red cabbage with balsamic:** Use red cabbage and drizzle with a little balsamic vinegar after roasting. You'll keep that gorgeous purple colour and its anthocyanins.
- **Miso-sesame:** Swap the paprika oil for 1 tablespoon white miso, 1 tablespoon sesame oil and 2 tablespoons olive oil. Finish with sesame seeds and spring onion.
- **Make it a meal:** Serve the wedges over a bowl of lentils, chickpeas or quinoa for extra protein and fibre.
- **Dairy-free:** Replace the yogurt sauce with a simple tahini, lemon and water dressing.

## A few gentle cautions

Cabbage is a very safe food for most people, but a couple of things are worth knowing:

- **Gas and bloating.** Large portions of cruciferous vegetables can cause wind in some people. If you're not used to eating much fibre, start with smaller portions and build up.
- **Blood thinners.** Some cruciferous greens are high in vitamin K, which affects blood clotting. If you take warfarin, talk to your doctor or dietitian about keeping your intake steady rather than cutting vegetables out.
- **Thyroid worries.** You may see claims online that cabbage harms the thyroid. Cleveland Clinic says there's little evidence that normal amounts affect thyroid function — but if you have a thyroid condition, your doctor can advise you.

## Storing leftovers

Keep leftover wedges in an airtight container in the fridge for up to 3 days. They won't be quite as crispy, but a few minutes in a hot oven or air fryer brings back most of the crunch. Chopped leftovers are also lovely tossed into a grain bowl or folded into an omelette.

## The bottom line

The 2026 cabbage trend is one of those rare food fads that's actually good for you and your wallet. Cabbage is low in calories, high in vitamin C and a decent source of fibre, and roasting turns it from boring to genuinely crave-worthy. It won't cure anything on its own, but adding more vegetables like this to your week is one of the simplest, best-supported habits for long-term health. Grab a cabbage, turn up the oven, and see what all the fuss is about.
