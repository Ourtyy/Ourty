If you start your day with a mug of tea, the headlines this autumn probably made you smile. A study following almost half a million adults found that regular tea drinkers had a lower risk of heart and blood vessel disease. Some news stories went further and talked about "6 to 8 cups a day."

So should you put the kettle on more often? Let's look at what the study actually found, where its limits are, and how to make your tea habit as heart-friendly as possible.

## What the new study looked at

The research was published in October 2026 in the *Journal of Internal Medicine* by a team led by Dr Zichen Ye at Peking Union Medical College. They used data from **485,934 adults in China** and followed them for a median of about nine years.

At the start, people answered questions about how often they drank tea, what type, how strong and how hot. Over the following years, the researchers counted who developed cardiovascular disease, stroke and other blood vessel conditions, such as peripheral artery disease. In total, **14,863 people** developed one of these conditions.

## The main findings

Here is what stood out:

- **Regular tea drinkers had about an 11% lower risk** of heart and blood vessel disease than people who didn't drink tea regularly.
- **Black tea** showed more consistent links with lower risk than green tea.
- **Moderate-strength tea** was linked with the lowest risk.
- **"Burning hot" tea** was linked with a *higher* risk, not a lower one.
- Among people who drank tea every week, the lowest risk appeared at about **6 to 8 cups a day**.
- The healthiest group of all combined three things: drinking tea, not smoking and being physically active.

That last point is worth remembering. Tea looked helpful, but it worked best as part of an overall healthy lifestyle, not as a replacement for one.

## What the study doesn't prove

This is an observational study. It spots patterns in a large group of people, but it can't prove that tea itself *caused* the lower risk. Tea drinkers may differ from non-tea drinkers in many ways, like diet, income or daily routines, and those differences are hard to fully account for.

Independent experts quoted by *Sci.News* were careful about this:

- Dr Charalampos Rallis of Queen Mary University of London pointed out that the 11% is a *relative* reduction, and that the authors themselves caution that "6–8 cups a day" should not be read as a recommended amount. He also called the black tea and hot tea findings exploratory.
- Dr Dell Stanford, a senior dietitian at the British Heart Foundation, said the study doesn't prove that a daily cuppa protects your heart.

There are other limits too. Tea intake was only measured once, at the start of the study, even though habits change over the years. Cup sizes weren't standardised, so "a cup" may mean very different amounts. And the study took place in China, where tea is often drunk plain and in different ways than in Europe or North America.

> **In short:** the study adds to a growing pile of evidence that tea is a healthy drink. It doesn't mean tea is medicine, and it doesn't mean you should drink eight cups a day.

## Why might tea be good for the heart?

Black and green tea both come from the same plant and contain natural plant compounds called **polyphenols**, including flavonoids. Researchers think these compounds may help keep blood vessels healthy, which could partly explain the link seen in studies like this one.

Plain tea also has a simpler advantage: it's a way to stay hydrated without sugar or additives. If a cup of tea replaces a sugary soft drink, that swap alone is good news for your heart.

## How much tea is sensible?

The "6 to 8 cups" figure got a lot of attention, but there's a practical catch: **caffeine**.

According to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), about **400 mg of caffeine a day** is an amount not generally linked with negative effects for most healthy adults. The FDA estimates a 12-ounce serving of black tea contains around 71 mg of caffeine and green tea around 37 mg, though it varies a lot with brand and brewing time.

That means several cups of tea can fit comfortably for many people, but if you also drink coffee, energy drinks or cola, the total adds up quickly. Too much caffeine can cause jitters, a racing heart or poor sleep.

Some people need to be more careful:

- **During pregnancy**, the NHS advises no more than **200 mg of caffeine a day**. It estimates about 75 mg in a cup of tea, so that's roughly two to three cups if you have no other caffeine. The NHS also suggests no more than four cups of herbal tea a day in pregnancy.
- If you're breastfeeding, have a heart rhythm problem, anxiety or trouble sleeping, or take medication, **talk to your doctor** about what's right for you.

## 6 tips for a heart-friendly tea habit

1. **Let it cool a little.** The study linked burning hot tea with higher risk. Give your mug a few minutes before you sip.
2. **Go easy on sugar.** A spoonful in every cup adds up over a day. Try cutting down gradually, or add a slice of lemon or a little cinnamon instead.
3. **Watch the extras.** Syrup-laden tea lattes and bubble teas are closer to desserts than to a plain cup of tea.
4. **Count your caffeine.** If you drink several cups, switch to decaf or caffeine-free herbal tea in the afternoon to protect your sleep.
5. **Pick what you enjoy.** Black tea had the most consistent results in this study, but green tea is also a healthy choice. The best tea is the one you'll happily drink instead of something sugary.
6. **Pair it with the basics.** The lowest risk in the study came with not smoking and staying active. Your cup of tea can be a pleasant part of a healthy routine, not the whole plan.

## The bottom line

A large, well-designed study suggests that people who drink tea regularly have a somewhat lower risk of heart and blood vessel disease, especially when tea is moderate in strength and not scalding hot. It's encouraging news, but it doesn't prove tea protects your heart on its own, and experts warn against treating "6 to 8 cups a day" as a target.

If you already enjoy tea, there's good reason to keep going: keep it not too hot, light on sugar and within a sensible caffeine limit. And if you don't like tea, there's no need to force it. Moving more, not smoking and eating well still matter far more than any single drink.
