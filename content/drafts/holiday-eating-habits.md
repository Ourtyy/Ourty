From Thanksgiving to New Year, the calendar fills up with big dinners, office parties, cookie tins and "just one more" glass of something festive. For many of us, that comes with a familiar mix of excitement and dread: we want to enjoy it all, but we don't want to spend January feeling sluggish and starting yet another diet.

Here's the good news: you don't need strict rules, calorie counting or skipping dessert to get through the season feeling good. A handful of small habits can make a real difference. First, though, let's clear up a common myth.

## The truth about holiday weight gain

You may have heard that people gain 5 or even 10 pounds over the holidays. The research tells a calmer story.

A well-known study by the US National Institutes of Health weighed 195 adults from autumn to early spring. On average, they gained just over **one pound**, and most of that (about 0.8 lb) arrived in the six weeks between Thanksgiving and New Year's Day. Fewer than 10% gained more than 5 pounds. Interestingly, participants thought they had gained about 3 pounds more than they actually had.

A larger 2016 study published in *The New England Journal of Medicine* followed nearly 3,000 adults in the US, Germany and Japan using smart scales. In all three countries, people were about 0.5% heavier 10 days after Christmas than 10 days before.

So far, so reassuring. The catch? The NIH team found that the holiday pound **wasn't lost** during the rest of the year, and the 2016 researchers noted that people tend to keep about half of their holiday gain. A pound a year doesn't sound like much, but over a decade or two it quietly adds up.

That's why the goal isn't to "survive" the holidays with willpower. It's to build a few easy habits that let you enjoy the food and the company, without that slow creep.

## 7 habits for happier holiday eating

### 1. Don't "save up" by skipping meals

It's tempting to skip breakfast and lunch before a big dinner. But Harvard's nutrition experts point out that going too long without eating leads to strong hunger, and strong hunger tends to grab the quickest, easiest food in sight. Harvard Health puts it simply: come to the table with an appetite, but not ravenously hungry.

**Try this:** have a normal breakfast and a light lunch with some protein and fibre, like eggs on wholegrain toast or a bean soup. You'll arrive at dinner hungry enough to enjoy it, but calm enough to choose.

### 2. Start with a smaller plate

Plate size quietly shapes how much we eat. Harvard's Nutrition Source suggests using a plate no larger than about 9 inches (23 cm) and filling it once.

**Try this:** scan the whole buffet before you serve yourself. Pick the dishes you really love (the ones you only get once a year) and skip the everyday ones you can have any time.

### 3. Slow down and savour

Eating slowly gives your body time to notice when it's satisfied. Harvard's Nutrition Source mentions a review of 68 studies in which slower eating was linked to eating less food, because people felt full sooner.

Mindful eating isn't a magic weight-loss tool on its own, and the research on body weight is mixed. But it has been linked to more pleasure from food and less distracted, "autopilot" eating, which is exactly what holiday tables need.

**Try this:** put your fork down between bites, chat between mouthfuls, and really taste that stuffing. Aim to stop when you feel comfortably satisfied rather than stuffed.

### 4. Take a short walk after the meal

This might be the simplest holiday habit of all. A review of seven studies, summarised by UCLA Health, found that even a **five-minute walk after eating** had a measurable effect on moderating blood sugar levels. The benefit showed up in the 60 to 90 minutes after the meal. Standing helped too, just less than walking.

**Try this:** suggest a family stroll around the block after dinner, before dessert. Or volunteer to clear the table and wash up. It all counts, and it beats slumping on the sofa.

### 5. Be mindful with festive drinks

Drinks are an easy place for extra calories to hide. The NHS notes that alcohol contains about 7 calories per gram, almost as many as fat. A standard glass of wine can contain up to 158 calories, a pint of strong lager up to 222, and a small glass of cream liqueur around 153. Mixers like cola and tonic add more.

Alcohol also tends to loosen good intentions around food.

**Try this:** alternate each alcoholic drink with a glass of water (an NHS tip), never drink on an empty stomach, and try a sparkling water with citrus and herbs as a festive alcohol-free option.

### 6. Protect your sleep

Late nights, travel and a packed social calendar can eat into sleep. The CDC recommends at least 7 hours a night for adults and says enough sleep helps you stay at a healthy weight and supports your metabolism. It also suggests avoiding large meals and alcohol close to bedtime.

**Try this:** leave a little gap between the last big meal and bed when you can, and keep your normal wake-up time as steady as possible, even on days off.

### 7. Let go of the guilt

One big dinner doesn't define your health; what you do most days does. Labelling foods as "good" or "bad" and then feeling guilty often leads to an all-or-nothing mindset: "I've already ruined it, so I might as well keep going."

**Try this:** enjoy the meal fully, then simply go back to your normal routine the next day. No "detox", no punishing workout, no skipping meals to make up for it. That calm reset is what keeps a single feast from turning into a six-week habit.

## A quick holiday-plate cheat sheet

- **Half the plate:** vegetables and salads (roasted sprouts, carrots, green beans, salads)
- **A quarter:** protein (turkey, fish, lentil roast, beans)
- **A quarter:** your favourite starchy sides (potatoes, stuffing, bread)
- **Dessert:** yes! Choose the one you love most and savour a normal slice

> **Tip:** Bringing a dish to a party? Make it a colourful vegetable side or salad. You'll know there's at least one fresh option on the table, and hosts always appreciate it.

## The bottom line

Holiday weight gain is usually smaller than people fear, around a pound, but it tends to stay with us year after year. You don't have to give up your favourite foods to avoid it. Eat regular meals, use a smaller plate, slow down, take a short walk after eating, go easy on the drinks, protect your sleep and drop the guilt. Small habits like these let you enjoy every bite of the season and start the new year feeling good. If you have diabetes or another health condition, talk to your doctor about planning for holiday meals.
