The clocks have gone back, the evenings are dark by late afternoon, and suddenly getting out of bed feels like climbing a mountain. If you feel a little flatter in November, you're far from alone. But there's a difference between a seasonal slump and a real seasonal depression — and knowing which one you're dealing with can help you choose the right kind of support.

Here's what trusted health organisations say about the winter blues, seasonal affective disorder (SAD), and the habits that genuinely help.

## What's the difference?

Think of it as a spectrum.

**The winter blues** are a mild, common dip in mood and energy during the colder, darker months. You might feel a bit sluggish, crave comfort food, or want to stay in more — but you can still work, see friends and enjoy things. Cleveland Clinic estimates that around 10–20% of Americans experience this milder version.

**Seasonal affective disorder (SAD)** is a type of depression that comes back at the same time every year, usually starting in late autumn or early winter and easing in spring. According to Cleveland Clinic, about 5% of US adults have it. The key difference is that SAD affects your daily life: how you feel, think, sleep and function. The US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) notes that episodes typically last four to five months, and that many people who have SAD don't realise it.

## Common signs of winter-pattern SAD

The NHS and NIMH describe symptoms that go beyond simply "feeling a bit down":

- A persistent low mood and losing interest in things you normally enjoy
- Feeling hopeless, guilty or worthless
- Sleeping much more than usual and still feeling tired
- Increased appetite, especially craving carbohydrates, sometimes with weight gain
- Pulling away from friends and family
- Difficulty concentrating, irritability or restlessness

SAD is usually diagnosed when these symptoms return at the same time of year for two years or more, according to the NHS. It tends to start in young adulthood, is more common in women, and is more frequent in people who live farther from the equator, where winter days are shortest.

## Why does it happen?

Scientists don't know the full answer yet, but the leading theories all revolve around light. NIMH explains that reduced sunlight may:

- **Lower serotonin activity**, a brain chemical involved in mood
- **Boost melatonin**, the hormone that makes you sleepy, so you feel drowsy for more of the day
- **Disrupt your body clock**, making it harder to adjust to the shorter days

Low vitamin D in winter has also been suggested as a factor, but NIMH says studies on vitamin D as a treatment have shown mixed results.

## Six evidence-based ways to feel brighter

Whether you have the mild winter blues or are simply trying to stay well through the season, these habits are recommended by the NHS, NIMH and Cleveland Clinic.

### 1. Get outside in daylight every day

Even on grey, cloudy days, outdoor light is far brighter than indoor lighting. A walk in the morning or at lunchtime is one of the easiest things you can do. Try sitting near a window when you're indoors, too.

### 2. Keep moving

Regular exercise supports mood all year round. Cleveland Clinic suggests about 30 minutes at least three times a week. Combine it with tip one — a brisk outdoor walk is a double win.

### 3. Protect your sleep routine

It's tempting to hibernate, but oversleeping can leave you groggier. The NHS recommends going to bed and waking up at consistent times, and avoiding screens right before bed.

### 4. Stay connected

Winter makes it easy to cancel plans and stay home. Make an effort to keep up with friends, family and regular activities. Cleveland Clinic even suggests planning a busier schedule so you spend less time hunkered down alone.

### 5. Eat for steady energy

Carbohydrate cravings are common in winter. Rather than fighting them with willpower alone, build meals around vegetables, whole grains, protein and healthy fats so your energy stays more stable. Leaning heavily on sugary or starchy snacks can leave you feeling flatter.

### 6. Go easy on alcohol

A drink might feel cosy, but both the NHS and Cleveland Clinic advise limiting alcohol, which can worsen low mood and interfere with sleep.

> **Tip:** Write your daylight walk into your calendar like a meeting. Treating it as a fixed appointment makes it much more likely to happen when the sofa is calling.

## What about light boxes?

Light therapy lamps are everywhere on social media right now. Here's what the evidence says.

NIMH describes light therapy as a standard treatment for winter-pattern SAD. It usually involves sitting near a bright lamp of about 10,000 lux — much brighter than normal home lighting — for roughly 15–45 minutes (sources vary), typically in the morning, through autumn and winter. Cleveland Clinic notes that some people notice improvements within days, while for others it takes about two weeks. The lamp should filter out UV light, and you don't stare directly into it.

The NHS takes a more cautious view: it doesn't usually offer light therapy because the evidence is limited, although many people find it helpful.

A few important safety points:

- **Check with a doctor first** if you have an eye condition, diabetes, bipolar disorder, or take medicines that make you sensitive to light.
- **Never use a tanning bed** as a substitute — they give off harmful UV rays.
- **A cheap "mood lamp" isn't the same thing.** Many decorative lights are nowhere near bright enough to have an effect.

## When to talk to a professional

Lifestyle habits are a great foundation, but they aren't always enough — and that's nothing to feel ashamed about. SAD is a real form of depression, and it responds well to treatment. Talking therapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) can help; NIMH reports that a version adapted for SAD worked about as well as light therapy, with benefits that seemed to last longer. Doctors may also discuss medication.

See a doctor if:

- Your low mood is affecting your work, relationships or daily life
- You notice the same pattern returning every winter
- Self-help steps aren't making a difference after a few weeks

If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, seek help immediately. In the US, call or text 988. In the UK, call 111 or contact Samaritans on 116 123. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## The bottom line

Feeling a little slower in winter is normal, and simple habits — daylight, movement, a steady routine and time with people you love — can make a real difference. But if your mood dips hard every year and starts to take over your life, it may be seasonal affective disorder, which is very treatable. You don't have to just wait for spring. Reach out, get some light, and be gentle with yourself while the days are short.
