"Smile, it might never happen." "Fake it till you make it." Most of us have heard some version of this advice, and plenty of wellness posts still promise that forcing a grin will trick your brain into feeling happier. A new study from the University of Florida adds an important twist: one smile can help a little, but smiling over and over because you *have* to may leave you feeling worse.

Here's what the research found, how it fits with older studies, and what it means for your everyday mood.

## The new study in a nutshell

Psychologist Nicholas Coles and his team ran an online experiment with about 1,200 adults. The results were published in the *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* and shared by the university in November 2026.

Participants looked at pictures chosen to stir up feelings. Some were pleasant (think kittens and flowers), others unpleasant (scenes of theft or animals being mistreated). For each picture, people either posed a smile on purpose or simply let their face react naturally. Some smiled once; others smiled ten times in a row. Half were also told they might lose part of their payment if they didn't follow the smiling instructions. (In reality, everyone was paid in full.) Afterwards, they rated how happy they felt.

The study was a "registered report", which means the researchers published their plan and predictions before collecting the data. That makes the results harder to spin after the fact.

## What they found

- **A single smile helped a bit with bad feelings.** When people looked at unpleasant pictures, one deliberate smile gave a small mood boost, even under the threat of losing money.
- **Repeating the smile wore the benefit down.** Smiling again and again made the boost fade. Combine repetition with pressure, and the benefit disappeared completely.
- **Forcing it during good moments backfired.** With pleasant pictures, one extra smile added little. Repeated or pressured smiling actually left people *less* happy than those who reacted naturally, and more irritated and angry.

Coles got the idea from his own past job in customer service, where he was told off for not smiling enough. He has said that being made to smile is very different from choosing to smile, and that we may spend too much effort chasing happiness and too little simply being content.

## Wait — didn't science say smiling makes you happy?

Yes, and that's what makes this study interesting. In 2022, Coles helped lead the **Many Smiles Collaboration**, a large project with nearly 3,900 people in 19 countries. Researchers who believed in the "facial feedback" idea teamed up with sceptics and agreed on one shared method.

They found that some ways of forming a smile — copying photos of smiling actors, or pulling the corners of your mouth up toward your cheeks — did produce a noticeable lift in happiness. (The famous "hold a pen in your teeth" trick didn't work as well.) Even then, the researchers were careful to say they couldn't be certain a smile *always* causes happiness.

So the two studies don't really contradict each other. Put together, they suggest:

- A **brief, voluntary** smile can nudge your mood a little.
- A **repeated, required** smile — the kind you put on because someone expects it — doesn't carry that benefit and can turn into irritation.

## Why forced smiling might backfire

The study can't say exactly why, but there are a few sensible ideas.

**It's tiring.** Holding an expression you don't feel takes attention and effort. Norman Farb, a psychologist at the University of Toronto Mississauga who wasn't involved in the research, told *Newsweek* the drop in mood may partly be an "annoyance effect": the frustration of a tedious, monitored task.

**It flattens real feelings.** Farb also noted that posing a smile tended to pull people back toward their starting mood, damping both good and bad reactions. That's a mixed blessing. You might feel a little less upset, but you might also enjoy a genuinely lovely moment a little less.

**Control matters.** Smiling because you want to lift your own mood is a choice. Smiling because your job depends on it is not. Coles argues that a sense of control may be the key difference.

## What the study can't tell us

It's worth being honest about the limits:

- It was a **short online session**, not a real workplace. It can't show whether years of forced smiling harm wellbeing — Farb said it "cannot speak to that claim".
- The **money threat was pretend**, so the pressure was milder than in a real job.
- Happiness and anger were **self-reported**, which is normal in this kind of research but not perfect.

Still, the results fit with a long line of research on "emotional labour" — the work of managing your feelings for your job — which has often linked it to stress and burnout.

## How to use this in real life

You don't need to stop smiling. You just don't need to force it. Here are a few ideas that respect both the research and your real emotions.

### Use a smile as a small reset, not a mask
If you're stuck in a grumpy moment, try one gentle, deliberate smile — or look at a photo of someone you love smiling. Notice whether it helps. If it doesn't, let it go.

### Name what you feel
Instead of covering a hard feeling with a grin, try putting it into words: "I'm frustrated", "I'm tired", "I'm disappointed." Naming a feeling doesn't make it bigger; it often makes it easier to handle.

### Notice "cheerfulness on demand"
If your job or family expects you to be upbeat all the time, it's normal for that to feel draining. Small breaks, an honest chat with a colleague, or a few minutes alone after a busy shift can help you reset.

### Build moods from the outside in
Lasting good moods tend to come from how we live, not how we hold our face. The NHS suggests five simple steps for mental wellbeing:

1. Connect with other people.
2. Be physically active.
3. Learn new skills.
4. Help others.
5. Pay attention to the present moment.

> **Tip:** Next time someone tells you to "cheer up", you're allowed to just say, "I'm okay — I'm just having a quiet day."

### Know when to get help
Feeling low, flat or irritable most days for more than a couple of weeks is not something a smile can fix. If that sounds familiar, talk to your doctor or a mental health professional.

## The bottom line

A smile is a nice tool, not a rule. One deliberate smile may give you a small lift, especially when something has upset you. But smiling over and over because you feel you *must* — at work, online or around other people — seems to cancel out that benefit and can leave you more irritated. Smile when you mean it, be honest about how you feel, and focus on the habits that build real contentment over time.
