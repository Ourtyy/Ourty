If you've reached for a second cup of coffee this morning and felt a little guilty, the headlines lately might cheer you up. A huge study that followed more than 130,000 people for up to 43 years found that people who drank two to three cups of caffeinated coffee a day were less likely to develop dementia. The story has been doing the rounds again this month, and coffee lovers everywhere are sharing it.

But "coffee prevents dementia" is not quite what the study said. Here's what the researchers actually found, what their results can't tell us, and how to think about your daily cup.

## What the study looked at

The research was published in *JAMA*, one of the world's leading medical journals, by a team from Harvard and Mass General Brigham. They used data from two of the longest-running health studies in the United States: the Nurses' Health Study and the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study.

Over the decades, participants regularly filled in detailed questionnaires about what they ate and drank. The researchers then tracked who went on to develop dementia, who noticed problems with memory and thinking, and how people scored on cognitive tests. In total, 131,821 people were included, and 11,033 of them developed dementia during follow-up.

## What they found

The main results were encouraging for coffee and tea drinkers:

- **Lower dementia risk.** People with the highest intake of caffeinated coffee had about an **18% lower risk** of dementia than those who drank little or none.
- **Tea counted too.** Tea drinkers had roughly a **14% lower risk**, according to the senior author's comments to CNN.
- **There was a sweet spot.** The strongest links were seen at **2–3 cups of caffeinated coffee** or **1–2 cups of tea** a day. Drinking more didn't add extra benefit, though it didn't seem harmful either.
- **Fewer memory worries.** Among higher coffee drinkers, 7.8% reported noticing a decline in their memory or thinking, compared with 9.5% of people who rarely drank it.
- **Genes didn't change the picture.** The link looked similar in people with a high or low genetic risk of dementia.
- **Decaf didn't show the same link.** This is one of the most interesting findings, because it hints that caffeine itself may play a role.

## Why it might work (in theory)

Nobody knows for sure yet. Coffee and tea contain caffeine and plant compounds called polyphenols, which may help reduce inflammation and cell damage in the body. Experts have also pointed out that coffee may help with blood pressure and oxidative stress, both of which matter for brain health. The fact that decaf didn't show a benefit makes caffeine a leading suspect, but the authors say more research is needed to confirm it.

## What the study can't tell us

This is the important part. The study was **observational**: it looked at patterns in people's habits, rather than randomly asking some people to drink coffee and others not to. That means it can show a link, but it **can't prove that coffee causes** the lower risk.

There are a few reasons to stay cautious:

- **Coffee drinkers may differ in other ways.** People who drink coffee or tea might have different diets, incomes or daily routines, and those differences could explain part of the result.
- **The effect is modest.** The researchers themselves stressed that the effect size is small. An 18% lower relative risk is meaningful across a big population, but it's not a shield.
- **The participants weren't typical.** Most were nurses and other health professionals, so the results may not apply equally to everyone.
- **Memory worries are self-reported.** Noticing that your memory isn't as sharp is not the same as having dementia.

Experts not involved in the study summed it up well: there's no reason to give up your morning coffee if you enjoy it, but no one should start drinking coffee purely to protect their brain. More is not better.

> **Tip:** Think of coffee as a possible small bonus on top of brain-healthy habits, not a replacement for them.

## How to enjoy coffee in a brain-friendly way

If you already drink coffee and feel fine with it, these simple habits help you get the most from it:

- **Stay within a sensible amount.** The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) says up to about **400 mg of caffeine a day** is generally not linked to negative effects in healthy adults. That's roughly two to three 12-ounce mugs of regular brewed coffee, though caffeine content varies a lot between brands and coffee shops.
- **Protect your sleep.** Too much caffeine can cause insomnia, jitters, a racing heart and an upset stomach. Good sleep matters for your brain, so try having your last cup earlier in the day.
- **Watch what goes in it.** The study looked at coffee and tea, not syrupy coffee-shop drinks. Large amounts of sugar and cream add up quickly.
- **Tea is a great option too.** If coffee makes you anxious, one or two cups of tea may be a gentler choice.
- **Know when to be careful.** People who are pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding, people with anxiety, insomnia or heart rhythm problems, and anyone on medications that interact with caffeine should check with a doctor first.

## What really protects your brain

The best-supported ways to lower dementia risk aren't found in a mug. The NHS points out that what's good for your heart is also good for your brain, and recommends:

- Eating a balanced, varied diet
- Keeping a healthy weight
- Staying physically active
- Drinking alcohol only within sensible limits
- Not smoking
- Keeping your blood pressure in a healthy range

Experts also mention good sleep habits as part of a brain-healthy routine — another reason not to let coffee keep you up at night.

If you're worried about your memory or someone else's, don't wait: talk to a doctor. They can look into what's going on and help you plan next steps.

## The bottom line

A large, long-running study linked two to three cups of caffeinated coffee (or one to two cups of tea) a day with a modestly lower risk of dementia. That's good news if you already enjoy your daily brew. But the study can't prove coffee is the cause, and the effect was small. Keep enjoying coffee in sensible amounts, protect your sleep, and focus on the big habits — movement, a healthy diet, good blood pressure and not smoking — that do the heavy lifting for your brain.
