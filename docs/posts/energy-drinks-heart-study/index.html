Are Energy Drinks Bad for Your Heart? What a New Study Found

A new American Heart Association study links energy drinks and stimulant supplements to heart scares in young adults. Here's what it means for you.

By The Ourty Editorial Team · November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

Energy drinks have gone from a niche gym product to an everyday habit. They sit next to the water at the supermarket checkout, come in pastel cans with "clean energy" labels, and for many people they've quietly replaced the morning coffee. Sales have more than doubled in a decade, passing $20 billion in 2024.

So when the American Heart Association (AHA) presented a study this month linking energy drinks and stimulant supplements to cardiac arrest and heart attacks in young adults, the headlines were dramatic. Let's slow down and look at what the research really showed, what it can't tell us, and what's a sensible approach if you enjoy a can now and then.

What the new study looked at

The research was presented at the AHA's Scientific Sessions 2026 in Chicago (6–9 November). The team, led by a medical student at the University of Virginia, dug into the US Food and Drug Administration's database of adverse-event reports. These are reports people, doctors and companies send in when someone has a health problem after using a food or supplement.

They looked at 28,345 reports from 2017 to 2025, from people aged 13 and over. Of those, 854 involved energy drinks or dietary supplements containing caffeine or other stimulants. That included ingredients like guarana, yerba mate, green tea and coffee extracts, synephrine and yohimbine. The researchers compared these with about 27,500 reports about products without stimulants, such as soft drinks, protein bars, smoothies and vitamins.

What it found

Compared with the non-stimulant products, reports involving energy drinks and stimulant supplements were:

  • about 11 times more likely to mention cardiac arrest
  • about 4.5 times more likely to mention a heart attack
  • about 4 times more likely to mention an irregular heartbeat or palpitations
  • nearly 3 times more likely to mention a racing heart
  • more likely to mention chest pain and fainting

Around 45% of the heart-related reports linked to stimulant products involved adults aged 18 to 34. That surprised many people, because we usually think of heart attacks as a problem of later life.

What the study can't tell us

This is where the headlines and the science part ways. The researchers and outside experts were very clear about the limits:

  • It shows a link, not cause and effect. The study does not prove that energy drinks caused any of these heart events.
  • The reports are voluntary. Nobody knows how many millions of people drank these products with no problems at all, or how many heart events were never reported. So you can't turn "11 times more likely to be mentioned" into "11 times more likely to happen to you". Industry groups made exactly this point in response.
  • Other factors weren't measured. People may have been mixing energy drinks with alcohol or drugs, using them right before hard exercise, or living with a heart condition nobody had diagnosed yet.
  • It's preliminary. The findings were presented as a conference abstract and haven't yet been published in full or peer-reviewed.

So this is a warning light, not a verdict. Gregory Marcus, a cardiologist at UC San Francisco who led the AHA's 2026 scientific statement on caffeine, said the study raises concerns that very high-caffeine products may harm the heart, while stressing that it doesn't prove they cause these events.

Why energy drinks are different from your coffee

Caffeine itself isn't the villain. For most healthy adults, up to about 400 mg of caffeine a day is generally considered safe, according to both the FDA and the AHA. That's roughly two to three large (12-ounce) mugs of brewed coffee, or up to five small 8-ounce cups.

The problem is how fast energy drinks and pre-workout powders can push you past that line:

  • Doses vary wildly. The FDA says energy drinks generally contain 54 to 328 mg of caffeine per 16-ounce can. One big can can be most of your daily limit.
  • They're stronger per sip. According to the AHA, some energy drinks have up to three times more caffeine per fluid ounce than brewed coffee, and some pre-workout supplements have more than 12 times as much.
  • They're easy to stack. A can at lunch, a pre-workout scoop at the gym, and a coffee in the morning can add up quickly. The study author noted that occasional use is probably low-risk, but regular users can easily exceed the daily limit.
  • They contain extra stimulants. Ingredients like guarana add more caffeine on top of what's on the label, and other stimulants can raise heart rate and blood pressure too.
  • They're often drunk fast and cold. Chugging a can before a workout is very different from sipping a hot coffee over half an hour.

The FDA also warns that very large amounts taken quickly (around 1,200 mg) can cause serious effects like seizures, which is why concentrated caffeine powders are especially risky.

Who should be most careful

  • Teens and children. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against energy drinks for children and teens, and the CDC notes it recommends adolescents stay under 100 mg of caffeine a day.
  • Anyone with a heart condition, high blood pressure or a history of irregular heartbeat. Cardiologists quoted in coverage of the study recommend avoiding energy drinks altogether in this case.
  • People with a family history of sudden cardiac death at a young age.
  • Anyone pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding, who should ask their doctor about caffeine limits.
  • People on medication, since some medicines make you more sensitive to caffeine.

If you're unsure, talk to your doctor before using energy drinks or pre-workout products.

Warning signs not to ignore

Too much caffeine can show up as a pounding or racing heart, jitters, anxiety, nausea, headache or trouble sleeping. If you ever have chest pain, fainting, or a fast or irregular heartbeat after an energy drink or supplement, stop and get medical help straight away. And tell your doctor what you drank: the study authors want health professionals to ask young patients with these symptoms about energy drinks.

Smarter ways to get your energy back

If you reach for a can because you're tired, the real fix usually sits somewhere else:

  1. Check your total caffeine. Add up coffee, tea, soda, energy drinks and pre-workout. Read labels; many cans list caffeine in milligrams.
  2. Pick lower-dose options. A cup of tea has far less caffeine than most energy drinks. Coffee sipped slowly is gentler than a can drunk in one go.
  3. Skip the stack. Avoid combining energy drinks with alcohol or with pre-workout supplements, and don't use them as a sports drink. Water is still the best way to hydrate during exercise.
  4. Set a caffeine curfew. Caffeine late in the day can wreck your sleep, which leaves you more tired tomorrow, which sends you back to the fridge for another can.
  5. Fix the energy dip at the source. Regular meals with protein and fibre, a short walk outside, and enough sleep do more for steady energy than any can.

The bottom line

The new AHA study doesn't prove that energy drinks cause heart attacks, and an occasional can is unlikely to harm a healthy adult. But it adds to growing concern that very high-caffeine drinks and pre-workout powders can push people past safe limits, especially young adults who use them often or mix them with alcohol or hard exercise. Keep your total caffeine under about 400 mg a day, keep energy drinks away from kids and teens, and if you have any heart concerns, talk to your doctor first.
