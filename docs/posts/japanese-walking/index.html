Japanese Walking: Does the 3-Minute Interval Trend Really Work?

Japanese walking alternates 3 minutes fast and 3 minutes slow. Here's what the research behind the viral trend shows, its limits, and how to start.

By The Ourty Editorial Team · November 13, 2026 · 5 min read

Walking has quietly become the coolest workout on the internet. And this year, one version has stood out above the rest: "Japanese walking". It has been one of the most talked-about fitness trends of 2026, all over social feeds and fitness trend lists. The idea is simple: walk fast for three minutes, slow down for three minutes, and repeat for half an hour.

Unlike many viral workouts, this one didn't start with an influencer. It comes from Japanese university researchers who have been studying it for close to two decades. So does it live up to the hype? Let's look at what the method is, what the research actually found, and how to try it safely — even on a grey November day.

What is Japanese walking?

Its proper name is interval walking training, and it was developed by researchers at Shinshu University in Japan. According to the university, the basic recipe is:

  • 3 minutes of brisk walking, fast enough that you're a bit out of breath. The researchers aim for roughly 70% of a person's peak aerobic capacity.
  • 3 minutes of slow, easy walking to recover.
  • Repeat five times, for 30 minutes in total.
  • Do it at least four days a week.

That's it. No equipment, no gym, no app required (though the university has made one). It's walking with a rhythm.

What the research says

The original study

The method became famous thanks to a randomised controlled trial from the Shinshu team. As described in an article republished from The Conversation, the study followed 246 adults with an average age of 63. One group did interval walking; another walked at a steady, moderate pace.

The interval walkers saw significantly bigger improvements in thigh muscle strength, aerobic fitness and blood pressure than the steady walkers. That's notable: leg strength and fitness tend to decline with age, and both matter for staying independent and active later in life.

Newer findings

The research hasn't stopped there. In 2025, Shinshu University reported a five-month study of 234 postmenopausal women. Bone mineral density improved in women who started with low bone density, while those with higher bone density stayed about the same. The university also reports fitness gains of up to 20% after five months of training, and links the method to a lower risk of lifestyle-related diseases.

A 2024 review, mentioned in The Conversation piece, concluded that the benefits seem to hold up across larger groups of middle-aged and older adults.

What we don't know yet

Here's the honest part:

  • It's not a longevity hack (yet). No study has directly shown that Japanese walking helps you live longer.
  • Not everyone sticks with it. In the original trial, about 22% of people dropped out of the interval programme — more than in the steady-walking group. Pushing hard, even for three minutes, isn't for everyone every day.
  • Much of the research comes from one team and mostly involves middle-aged and older adults in Japan. It's promising, but more independent studies would help.

Our take: Japanese walking isn't magic. It's a smart, evidence-based way to add some intensity to a habit you may already have. That's exactly why it's worth trying.

Why intervals can make walking more effective

Ordinary walking is excellent for you. But once your body gets used to it, a gentle stroll stops being much of a challenge. Short bursts of faster walking raise your heart rate and make your leg muscles work harder, and the slow minutes give you time to recover so you can do it again.

It also fits neatly into official advice. The NHS recommends 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and brisk walking counts. Four 30-minute interval sessions take you most of the way there. (If you want the full picture on activity targets, see our guide to how much exercise you really need.)

How to try Japanese walking

Find your "fast" and "slow"

You don't need a heart-rate monitor. Use the talk test:

  • Fast minutes: the NHS describes brisk walking as a pace where you can still talk but can't sing. For Japanese walking, push a little harder — talking should feel like an effort.
  • Slow minutes: an easy, comfortable stroll where you could chat freely.

A simple first session

  1. Warm up with a few minutes of easy walking.
  2. Set a timer on your phone or watch to beep every 3 minutes.
  3. Alternate fast and slow, starting with a fast block, for 30 minutes.
  4. Cool down with a few relaxed minutes and a gentle stretch.

Make it work for you

  • Start smaller. If 30 minutes feels like a lot, begin with three or four rounds (18–24 minutes) and build up over a few weeks. The NHS advises increasing gradually if you're not very active.
  • Swing your arms and take quick, purposeful steps during the fast minutes.
  • Use the terrain. A gentle hill during the brisk minutes adds extra challenge.
  • Wear supportive shoes that don't rub — the NHS lists comfortable footwear as a walking essential.
  • Beat the winter gloom. Darker afternoons make it tempting to skip. Try a lunchtime walk to catch some daylight, wear something reflective after dark, or do intervals on a treadmill if the weather turns.
  • Bring company. A friend, a podcast or a playlist with a change of pace every three minutes keeps it fun.

Safety first: If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, joint problems or haven't been active for a while, talk to your doctor before starting any new exercise routine. Stop and seek advice if you feel chest pain, dizziness or unusual breathlessness.

The bottom line

Japanese walking is a rare viral trend that's built on real research. Alternating three minutes of brisk walking with three minutes of easy walking has been shown to improve leg strength, fitness and blood pressure more than walking at a steady pace, at least in middle-aged and older adults. It won't add years to your life overnight, and it isn't the only way to stay active. But if you already enjoy walking and want more from it, setting a 3-minute timer is one of the easiest upgrades you can make.
