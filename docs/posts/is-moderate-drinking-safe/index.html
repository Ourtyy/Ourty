Is Moderate Drinking Safe? What a Big New Alcohol Study Found

A major 2026 study found no level of drinking that's good for your overall health. Here's what it means for your holiday glass of wine.

By The Ourty Editorial Team · November 19, 2026 · 5 min read

For years, a glass of red wine with dinner had a healthy halo. Maybe it was good for your heart? Maybe moderate drinkers lived longer? Many of us grew up hearing some version of that idea.

A large study published in the Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs this year adds to a growing pile of evidence that says otherwise. It made headlines again this month, just as the season of office parties, festive dinners and New Year toasts begins. So let's look at what the researchers actually found, what it doesn't mean, and how to think about your own drinking without guilt or panic.

What the study did

This wasn't a single group of people followed over time. Instead, a team led by researchers from the University of Toronto, Columbia University and the Public Health Institute in California did something bigger: they reviewed more than 7,200 scientific papers on alcohol and disease, then combined those findings with large US health datasets.

Using statistical models, they estimated the lifetime risk of dying early or getting ill from alcohol at different drinking levels. They included conditions where alcohol has a well-established causal link, such as liver disease, injuries, heart disease and cancers of the mouth, oesophagus and breast.

The key findings

  • No net benefit at any level. The researchers found no amount of drinking that gave an overall protective effect on health.
  • The heart story is only half the picture. Low intake was linked to a possibly lower risk of one type of heart disease and stroke. But those possible benefits were outweighed by the extra risk of cancer, liver disease and injuries, even at around seven drinks a week.
  • Risk climbs above about one drink a day. At roughly seven drinks a week, the study estimated about one alcohol-related death per 1,000 people over a lifetime, for both men and women. Above that, risk rose more steeply.
  • Two drinks a day is not "low risk". At 14 drinks a week, which until recently was the upper limit for men in US dietary advice, the estimated lifetime risk of dying from an alcohol-related cause for men was about 1 in 25.

The researchers also found that drinking a lot in one sitting raised risks on its own, regardless of the weekly total. In other words, saving all your drinks for Saturday night is not the same as spreading a few across the week.

Why the "healthy drinker" idea is fading

This study doesn't stand alone. The World Health Organization has said since 2023 that there is no safe level of alcohol for health, because risk "starts from the first drop". Alcohol is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen, the same category as tobacco and asbestos.

The US National Cancer Institute explains why: the body breaks alcohol down into a chemical called acetaldehyde, which can damage DNA. Alcohol is strongly linked to cancers of the mouth, throat, oesophagus, liver, bowel and breast. Even about one drink a day is linked to a higher breast cancer risk in women compared with drinking very rarely.

So why did older studies suggest moderate drinkers were healthier? WHO points out that those results depend heavily on who is compared with whom. "Non-drinkers" in some studies included people who had stopped drinking because they were already ill, which made moderate drinkers look better by comparison.

What this study does not say

It's worth being clear about the limits, too:

  • It's a model, not a crystal ball. The numbers are population estimates. Your own risk depends on genetics, family history, overall lifestyle and how you drink.
  • Small amounts carry small risks. One in 1,000 over a lifetime is real but modest. Nobody is saying a glass of champagne at a wedding is dangerous.
  • Some links are still being studied. The authors only included conditions with a clear causal link; others, such as pancreatic cancer, need more research.

The honest takeaway: drinking less is better for health, and there's no need to start drinking for your heart. If you enjoy a drink, the goal is to make it a conscious choice rather than a health habit.

How to drink less this season (without missing out)

The UK's NHS advises keeping to 14 units a week or less if you drink, spreading them over three or more days and having several drink-free days. For reference, a standard 175ml glass of wine is about 2 units, and a pint of stronger beer is about 3, so it adds up faster than many people expect.

Some simple ideas that actually work:

  1. Decide before you go. Pick your number (say, one or two drinks) before the party, not halfway through it.
  2. Alternate. Have a glass of sparkling water, a soda with lime or an alcohol-free drink between alcoholic ones.
  3. Go smaller. Choose a small glass of wine instead of a large one, or a bottle of beer instead of a pint.
  4. Plan drink-free days. Make a few evenings a week alcohol-free by default, so drinking becomes the exception.
  5. Upgrade the alternatives. Alcohol-free options have come a long way. A festive mocktail with cranberry, rosemary and soda feels just as celebratory.
  6. Notice how you feel. Many people find they sleep better and wake up with more energy on the days they skip alcohol.

Tip: If you're thinking about a "Dry January" or "Damp January", the weeks before are a great time to experiment. Try one alcohol-free weekend in December and see how it feels.

When to talk to a doctor

If you find it hard to cut back, drink to cope with stress, or feel unwell when you stop, talk to your doctor. That's also wise if you have liver problems, take medicines that interact with alcohol, are pregnant or trying to conceive, or have a family history of alcohol-related cancers. Support is available and nothing to be embarrassed about.

The bottom line

The idea that moderate drinking is good for you is fading fast. This large 2026 study found no level of alcohol that improves overall health, with risks rising noticeably above about one drink a day. That doesn't mean a festive toast is off-limits. It means alcohol is best treated as an occasional treat, not a wellness habit, and that drinking a little less is one of the simplest things you can do for your long-term health.
